Watts vs kWh: how appliance electricity costs work

Watts tell you how much power an appliance can draw. Kilowatt-hours tell you how much energy it uses over time. That distinction is the key to understanding an electricity bill and estimating what an appliance costs to run.

Quick answer

What is the difference between watts and kWh?

Watts (W) measure power at a moment. Kilowatts (kW) are watts divided by 1,000. Kilowatt-hours (kWh) measure energy: the amount of power used over a period of time. A 2,000 W appliance is a 2 kW appliance; if it drew 2 kW continuously for one hour, it would use 2 kWh.

In real homes, an appliance often does not draw its rated power continuously. A heater may cycle, a fridge compressor switches on and off, and a tumble dryer can reduce heating as clothes dry. This is why a rating label and a measured kWh figure answer different questions.

Worked calculation

How do you convert watts to kWh?

Start by converting watts to kilowatts: divide by 1,000. Then multiply by the number of hours the appliance runs. For a simple mathematical example, a 2,000 W appliance used for 2 hours per day is 2 kW × 2 hours = 4 kWh per day. Used seven days per week, that is 28 kWh per week.

Why numbers differ

Rated power is not the same as actual consumption

A rating is useful for understanding an appliance's maximum or nominal electrical demand. It does not tell you exactly how many kWh the appliance will use during a whole programme. Actual use can change with thermostat cycling, motor load, sensor controls, temperature, programme length, load size and standby behaviour.

Manufacturer energy labels may report a tested programme or standardised annual consumption rather than multiplying the maximum wattage by an assumed number of hours. That makes labels useful for comparing eligible appliances, but the result may not match a simple wattage calculation using your own routine.

Common mistakes

Four easy watts-and-kWh mistakes

Confusing W with Wh

Watts are power. Watt-hours and kilowatt-hours are energy. A number in watts has no time period attached.

Forgetting the 1,000 conversion

2,000 W is 2 kW, not 2,000 kW. Divide watts by 1,000 before multiplying by hours.

Assuming rated power is constant

An appliance rated at 2,000 W may cycle below that level. Treat a rating as an input assumption unless you have measured consumption.

Ignoring the tariff

The same kWh use costs different amounts on different tariffs. Enter your own p/kWh rate in the calculator when accuracy matters.
