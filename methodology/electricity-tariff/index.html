How the numbers work

Electricity cost calculator methodology

Understand how WattCost UK turns appliance power, usage and an electricity tariff into a running-cost estimate, and why the result may differ from your bill.

The basics

Watts, kilowatts and kWh

Watts (W) describe an appliance’s power at a point in time. One kilowatt (kW) is 1,000 watts. Kilowatt-hours (kWh) measure energy used over time: a 2 kW appliance running for one hour would use 2 kWh if it drew that power continuously.

Rated power is not always the same as measured consumption. Heating elements can switch on and off, motors can cycle, and sensors can change a programme’s power draw. Use the appliance label or an energy monitor when you need a measured figure.

The estimate

How running cost is calculated

WattCost UK converts the entered wattage to kilowatts, applies the entered hours and days, and prices the resulting energy using the entered pence-per-kWh tariff. The calculator engine performs this calculation for hourly, daily, weekly, monthly and annual results.

Annual figures use the site’s 52-week model: weekly energy is multiplied by 52. Monthly cost is the annual cost divided by 12. This keeps the displayed periods mathematically consistent with one another.

Tariff transparency

Which electricity price does WattCost UK use?

The default is an Ofgem-based illustrative unit rate held centrally in assets/js/data.js. It is intended as a starting point, not a price that every household pays. Actual rates vary by supplier, tariff, region, payment method, meter arrangement and, for some tariffs, time of use.

Ofgem explains that the price cap and standing charges can vary by location, payment method, fuel type and meter. The calculator does not add a standing charge because it estimates the marginal electricity cost of using an appliance. Replace the default with your own unit rate for a closer estimate.

See Ofgem’s unit rates and standing charges guidance.

Limits and review

What the calculator does and does not claim

The result is an estimate based on the numbers entered. It does not predict a whole energy bill, include standing charges, account for every appliance control cycle, or replace a manufacturer’s measured kWh figure. Appliance presets are typical starting points and should be adjusted where the rating plate, manual or energy label provides better information.

The tariff configuration includes an effective date and review date so it can be checked as Ofgem updates its quarterly price-cap information. Appliance assumptions require separate research and should not be published as authoritative data until they have a genuine source.
